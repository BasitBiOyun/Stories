# B2 TEACHER GUIDE  
## for the Interactive Storybook: Prophet Abraham

---

## 1. OVERVIEW

### Purpose of the Book

This interactive digital storybook helps **B2 learners** develop English through an extended faith-based narrative, visuals, audio, hotspots, guided discussion, close reading, structured reflection, and higher-level speaking and writing tasks.

The story of Prophet Abraham (as) offers rich material for language learning because it includes reasoning, debate, moral courage, migration, family trust, sacrifice, and the building of the Ka‘ba. At B2 level, students should not only understand the events, but also explain motives, compare ideas, evaluate arguments, and express personal reflections with clear supporting reasons.

### Target Learners

- Learners studying English around **CEFR B2**
- Students who can handle longer narrative texts, guided analysis, and values-based discussion
- Learners who benefit from combining language practice with reflective, meaningful content

### Language Level

**CEFR B2**

The language may include abstract vocabulary, cause-and-effect relationships, reported speech, argumentation, and reflective statements. Support should still be provided through visuals, audio, hotspots, and guided questions, but students should be encouraged to move beyond basic comprehension into interpretation and explanation.

### Main Learning Focus

- Extended reading and listening comprehension  
- Narrative sequencing across a long text  
- Abstract and thematic vocabulary  
- Argumentation and evidence-based discussion  
- Character and values analysis  
- Summary, reflection, comparison, and opinion writing  
- Speaking fluency through debate, retelling, and presentation tasks  

### Educational Value

The story gives learners meaningful English for discussing:

- Tawheed and monotheism  
- Hanifism and the path of Prophet Abraham (as)  
- critical thinking and the search for truth  
- courage in the face of social pressure  
- respectful advice and family conflict  
- arrogance, authority, and false power  
- trust in Allah during hardship  
- patience, sacrifice, gratitude, and obedience  
- the spiritual meaning of Zamzam, sacrifice, and the Ka‘ba  

### Digital Support

The digital format supports comprehension and classroom interaction.

- Hotspots clarify key terms, names, places, and concepts  
- Audio supports pronunciation, pacing, and listening fluency  
- Interactive checks help students monitor comprehension  
- Visuals support prediction, inference, and emotional engagement  
- Chapter activities allow students to recycle vocabulary and ideas

## 2. CURRICULUM ALIGNMENT

This guide supports a holistic classroom approach by combining language learning, structured participation, critical reflection, and values education.

### Language Skills

- **Listening & Viewing:** students follow audio, visuals, and narration to understand tone, sequence, and emphasis  
- **Reading Comprehension:** students identify main ideas, supporting details, cause-effect links, motives, and implied meanings  
- **Spoken Interaction:** students discuss, defend opinions, compare characters, ask follow-up questions, and respond respectfully  
- **Spoken Production:** students retell episodes, present moral lessons, and explain abstract concepts in organized speech  
- **Writing:** students produce summaries, reflections, paragraph responses, comparison tasks, and short argumentative texts  
- **Vocabulary Development:** students learn thematic and academic words in context and reuse them in speaking and writing  

### Personal and Social Development

- **Values Education:** sincerity, courage, trust, patience, gratitude, humility, obedience, sacrifice, responsibility  
- **Social-Emotional Learning:** identifying fear, pressure, anger, arrogance, hope, trust, and perseverance  
- **Reflective Thinking:** connecting story lessons to real-life decision-making, leadership, and personal integrity  
- **Critical Thinking:** evaluating claims, false authority, inherited beliefs, and the logic of worshipping created things

## 3. TEACHING APPROACH

The teaching approach should be interactive, reflective, and text-based. At B2 level, students should be guided to notice language and meaning together.

### Core Principles

- Use visuals and prediction before reading  
- Pre-teach only essential terms; let students infer the rest from context  
- Move from event comprehension to interpretation and evaluation  
- Ask students to support answers with evidence from the text  
- Use pair and group talk before whole-class discussion  
- Encourage students to distinguish fact, inference, and personal response  
- Recycle key vocabulary in multiple tasks  
- Balance religious sensitivity with open, respectful language practice  
- End each lesson with a short written or oral reflection  

### Recommended Classroom Sequence

1. **See it** – students observe the visual and predict the theme  
2. **Hear it** – students listen for tone, sequence, and main ideas  
3. **Read it** – students read closely and identify key lines  
4. **Analyze it** – students discuss motives, arguments, and values  
5. **Say it** – students retell, debate, or present  
6. **Write it** – students produce a short written response  
7. **Connect it** – students link the lesson to real-life character and faith  

### Teacher Role

The teacher should act as a facilitator of meaning, language, and reflection. The aim is not to lecture through the story, but to help students understand the text deeply, use English actively, and express mature ideas with clarity and respect.

## 4. IMPLEMENTATION PLANS

### Option A: Intensive Workshop (90 Minutes)

- 0–10 min: Visual warm-up and prediction  
- 10–25 min: Guided reading/listening of selected chapters  
- 25–40 min: Vocabulary and hotspot exploration  
- 40–55 min: Close reading and evidence questions  
- 55–70 min: Group discussion or debate  
- 70–85 min: Reflection writing or short presentation  
- 85–90 min: Exit ticket and review  

### Option B: Standard Split (3 x 40 Minutes)

- **Lesson 1:** Abraham’s mission, Tawheed, Hanifism, and the search for truth  
- **Lesson 2:** Idols, public debate, the fire miracle, and Nimrod  
- **Lesson 3:** Migration, Hajar and Ishmael, Zamzam, sacrifice, and the Ka‘ba  

### Option C: Extended Unit (6–8 Lessons)

- Deep reading of the full story  
- Vocabulary recycling and concept mapping  
- Character and values analysis  
- Debate on inherited beliefs and critical thinking  
- Timeline and map-based story reconstruction  
- Reflective writing portfolio  
- Final speaking project or group presentation

## 5. BEFORE / DURING / AFTER READING FRAMEWORK

### Before Reading

- Use the cover, title, or main illustration for prediction  
- Introduce 5–8 key words or concepts  
- Ask students to identify what they already know about Prophet Abraham (as)  
- Activate background knowledge through respectful, open-ended prompts  
- Set a reading purpose, such as “Find Abraham’s strongest argument” or “Notice how trust is shown”  

### During Reading

- Read or play the text in manageable chunks  
- Pause for gist, vocabulary, and inference questions  
- Ask students to underline key lines or write margin notes  
- Encourage students to infer before using hotspots  
- Use think-pair-share before whole-class answers  
- Ask students to identify cause-effect relationships  
- Recycle target vocabulary in oral questions  

### After Reading

- Reconstruct the timeline  
- Discuss the central value of the section  
- Ask one text-based interpretation question  
- Ask one personal or real-life connection question  
- Finish with a short exit task: summary, sentence completion, reflection, or oral response

## Module 1: Tawheed, Hanifism, and Abraham’s Mission

**Timing:** 40 minutes

### Learning Objectives

By the end of the lesson, students will be able to:

- explain the meaning of Tawheed and Hanifism in clear English  
- identify Abraham’s central mission in the story  
- distinguish between monotheism, idolatry, and inherited belief  
- summarize the introduction using 4–5 key terms  

### Expected Student Outcomes

Students should be able to:

- define Tawheed in one or two sentences  
- explain why Abraham (as) is presented as a major figure of monotheism  
- use key terms such as monotheism, idolatry, mission, representative, and faith  

### Key Vocabulary

Tawheed, Hanifism, monotheism, idolatry, idolater, representative, mission, corrupted, faith, guidance

### Pedagogical Focus

Use concept mapping. Students should see how Abraham’s story is not only a sequence of events, but also a story about belief, reasoning, and moral direction.

### Grammar / Discourse Focus

Definitions and explanation language:

- Tawheed means …  
- Hanifism refers to …  
- Abraham is presented as … because …  
- The central idea is that …  

### Pronunciation Focus

- word stress in **monotheism**, **representative**, **idolatry**, **corrupted**  
- clear pronunciation of names and terms: **Abraham**, **Ishmael**, **Isaac**, **Tawheed**

### Materials / App Features

- opening pages of the story  
- key term hotspots  
- concept map or board diagram  
- audio playback  
- short definition-matching task  

### 40-Minute Lesson Flow

**0–5 min | Warm-up**  
Ask: “What makes a belief central to a person’s life?”  
Ask students to brainstorm words connected to faith and truth.

**5–10 min | Vocabulary Preview**  
Introduce Tawheed, Hanifism, monotheism, and idolatry with simple examples.

**10–18 min | Guided Reading / Listening**  
Read or listen to the introduction in short chunks. Students note three key ideas.

**18–25 min | Concept Map**  
Create a class map with Abraham (as) at the center and branches for Tawheed, mission, prophets, and struggle.

**25–32 min | Pair Explanation**  
Students explain one key term to a partner using a sentence frame.

**32–37 min | Reflection Discussion**  
Ask: “Why does the story begin with belief before action?”

**37–40 min | Exit Ticket**  
Students write one definition and one sentence about Abraham’s mission.

### Teacher Language

- “Use the text to support your answer.”  
- “Can you explain that in your own words?”  
- “What is the difference between worshipping Allah and worshipping created things?”  
- “Turn the key word into a full sentence.”  

### Comprehension Questions

- How is Prophet Abraham (as) presented in the introduction?  
- What is Tawheed?  
- Why is Hanifism connected to Abraham (as)?  

### Discussion Points

- Why do people sometimes follow inherited beliefs without questioning them?  
- What makes a belief sincere rather than only cultural?  
- Why is reasoning important in faith?  

### Possible Difficulties

- Students may understand the terms in their first language but struggle to explain them in English  
- Abstract vocabulary may feel heavy at first  
- Students may summarize too generally without using textual evidence  

### Support Strategies

- Provide definition frames  
- Use a concept map instead of long explanations  
- Accept partial definitions first, then refine them  

### Differentiation

- **Fast finishers:** write a 4-sentence summary of the introduction  
- **Struggling learners:** match key terms with short definitions  

### Interactive App Tips

- Use hotspots for Tawheed, Hanifism, and monotheism before discussion  
- Replay audio for difficult sentences and ask students to notice emphasis  

### Assessment Tools

- Exit ticket: one definition and one mission sentence  
- Teacher observation: use of key vocabulary and clarity of explanation  

### Kinesthetic Activity

Students stand in different corners labeled “belief,” “action,” “reason,” and “trust.” They choose the corner that best connects to Abraham’s mission and explain why.

### Global Citizenship Link

How can critical thinking help people avoid blind imitation and make responsible choices?

### Homework / Extension

Write a short paragraph: “Why is Abraham’s search for truth important?”

## Module 2: Childhood, Idols, and Critical Thinking

**Timing:** 40 minutes

### Learning Objectives

By the end of the lesson, students will be able to:

- describe Abraham’s childhood attitude toward idols  
- explain why idols are shown as powerless in the story  
- identify contrast between appearance and reality  
- use cause-and-effect language to explain Abraham’s reasoning  

### Expected Student Outcomes

Students should be able to:

- retell the childhood episode in sequence  
- explain Abraham’s reaction to Mardukh and the temple  
- use cause-effect connectors such as because, therefore, as a result, and this shows that  

### Key Vocabulary

idol, statue, temple, worship, lifeless, helpless, priest, bow, beg, offering, ridicule, reasoning

### Pedagogical Focus

Use guided discovery and visual contrast. Students should compare what the people believed the idols could do with what the idols could actually do.

### Grammar / Discourse Focus

Cause and effect:

- Abraham laughed because …  
- The idols could not move; therefore, …  
- This shows that …  
- As a result, Abraham rejected …  

### Pronunciation Focus

- final consonants in **lifeless**, **helpless**, **worshipped**  
- stress in **ridiculous**, **offering**, **authority**

### Materials / App Features

- idol and temple visuals  
- hotspots for idol, statue, temple, worship  
- comparison chart  
- audio playback  
- short cause-effect sentence task  

### 40-Minute Lesson Flow

**0–5 min | Warm-up**  
Ask: “Can something made by a person become more powerful than the person who made it?”

**5–10 min | Visual Prediction**  
Show an image of a temple or idol scene. Students predict the conflict.

**10–18 min | Guided Reading / Listening**  
Focus on Abraham’s father, Mardukh, and the temple.

**18–25 min | Contrast Chart**  
Students complete: “People thought idols could…” / “The idols actually could…”

**25–32 min | Cause-Effect Practice**  
Students write or say three cause-effect sentences.

**32–37 min | Discussion**  
Ask: “Why does the story use Abraham’s childhood to show clear thinking?”

**37–40 min | Exit Ticket**  
Students complete: “Abraham rejected idols because…”

### Teacher Language

- “What did the people believe?”  
- “What does Abraham notice?”  
- “Use because or therefore.”  
- “What is the evidence in the story?”  

### Comprehension Questions

- What did Abraham’s father make?  
- Why did Abraham laugh at the statue of Mardukh?  
- What did people do inside the temple?  

### Discussion Points

- Why can appearances be misleading?  
- Why is it important to question false ideas respectfully but clearly?  
- What is the difference between respect and worship?  

### Possible Difficulties

- Students may retell events without explaining the logic  
- Words such as lifeless, helpless, and offering may need support  
- Students may need help expressing contrast in English  

### Support Strategies

- Use a two-column chart  
- Provide connectors on the board  
- Model one strong explanation sentence  

### Differentiation

- **Fast finishers:** write a short internal monologue for young Abraham  
- **Struggling learners:** match verbs to objects: bow to, make, worship, break, question  

### Interactive App Tips

- Use idol hotspots to review vocabulary  
- Ask students to replay audio and identify Abraham’s attitude  

### Assessment Tools

- Cause-effect sentence quality  
- Participation in contrast chart  
- Exit ticket sentence  

### Kinesthetic Activity

Students act out “appearance” and “reality” as frozen poses, then explain the difference in one sentence.

### Global Citizenship Link

How can people respectfully challenge ideas that harm clear thinking?

### Homework / Extension

Write five sentences comparing what people believed about the idols and what Abraham understood.

## Module 3: The Sky, Reasoning, and the Search for Allah

**Timing:** 40 minutes

### Learning Objectives

By the end of the lesson, students will be able to:

- summarize Abraham’s reflection on the star, moon, and sun  
- explain why created things cannot be the Creator  
- identify repeated reasoning patterns in the passage  
- use contrastive structures to explain belief  

### Expected Student Outcomes

Students should be able to:

- describe the sequence of star, moon, and sun  
- explain the meaning of “created things” and “Creator”  
- use although, however, and but in short explanations  

### Key Vocabulary

creation, Creator, disappear, set, command, certainty, heavenly body, reasoning, evidence, worship

### Pedagogical Focus

Use pattern recognition. Students should notice that the star, moon, and sun are tested by the same logic: they appear, disappear, and are therefore not worthy of worship.

### Grammar / Discourse Focus

Contrast and concession:

- Although the sun is bright, it sets.  
- The moon looks powerful; however, it disappears.  
- Created things change, but Allah does not.  

### Pronunciation Focus

- vowel sounds in **Creator** and **creation**  
- stress in **disappear**, **certainty**, **heavenly**

### Materials / App Features

- sky visuals  
- star, moon, and sun hotspots  
- audio playback  
- reasoning pattern worksheet  
- short explanation task  

### 40-Minute Lesson Flow

**0–5 min | Warm-up**  
Ask: “What changes in the sky during one day?”

**5–10 min | Vocabulary Preview**  
Teach appear, disappear, set, created, Creator.

**10–18 min | Guided Reading / Listening**  
Read the sky reflection passage in three parts: star, moon, sun.

**18–25 min | Pattern Task**  
Students complete a table: object / what Abraham saw / what happened / conclusion.

**25–32 min | Language Practice**  
Students write contrast sentences using although, however, or but.

**32–37 min | Reflection**  
Ask: “How does reasoning help Abraham reach certainty?”

**37–40 min | Exit Ticket**  
Students write one sentence explaining why created things cannot be Allah.

### Teacher Language

- “What is repeated in this passage?”  
- “What changes and what does not change?”  
- “Use a contrast word.”  
- “What conclusion does Abraham reach?”  

### Comprehension Questions

- What did Abraham see first?  
- Why did he reject the star, moon, and sun as objects of worship?  
- What did he understand about Allah?  

### Discussion Points

- What is the difference between a sign and the Creator of the sign?  
- Why does the story show Abraham thinking deeply?  
- How can observation lead to belief?  

### Possible Difficulties

- Students may confuse “worship the sun” with “observe the sun”  
- Abstract terms may need repeated examples  
- Contrast structures may be inaccurate  

### Support Strategies

- Use a visual sequence  
- Provide sentence stems  
- Let students rehearse orally before writing  

### Differentiation

- **Fast finishers:** write a short paragraph explaining Abraham’s reasoning  
- **Struggling learners:** complete a guided sentence table  

### Interactive App Tips

- Use hotspots for star, moon, sun, and Creator  
- Replay audio and ask students to listen for repeated question forms  

### Assessment Tools

- Reasoning table  
- Contrast sentences  
- Exit ticket explanation  

### Kinesthetic Activity

Students move from “star” to “moon” to “sun” stations and say the repeated reasoning pattern at each station.

### Global Citizenship Link

How can careful observation help people make wiser decisions?

### Homework / Extension

Write a six-sentence explanation of Abraham’s reflection on the sky.

## Module 4: Public Debate, Broken Idols, and Moral Courage

**Timing:** 40 minutes

### Learning Objectives

By the end of the lesson, students will be able to:

- retell the idol-breaking episode in order  
- explain Abraham’s purpose in leaving the largest idol unbroken  
- evaluate Abraham’s public argument against idolatry  
- use reported speech and rhetorical questions in discussion  

### Expected Student Outcomes

Students should be able to:

- put the main events in correct sequence  
- explain how Abraham used the largest idol to expose false belief  
- identify the role of public questioning in the scene  

### Key Vocabulary

debate, challenge, smash, false god, culprit, trial, authority, ashamed, arrogant, admit, evidence

### Pedagogical Focus

Use dramatic sequencing and argument analysis. Students should understand that Abraham’s action is not random destruction; it is presented as a public demonstration of the idols’ helplessness.

### Grammar / Discourse Focus

Reported speech and rhetorical questions:

- Abraham asked why they worshipped lifeless objects.  
- He said that the biggest idol had done it.  
- How can something that cannot speak be a god?  

### Pronunciation Focus

- stress in **authority**, **arrogant**, **evidence**  
- intonation in rhetorical questions  

### Materials / App Features

- idol house visuals  
- sequencing cards  
- hotspots for axe, temple, biggest idol  
- audio playback  
- debate task  

### 40-Minute Lesson Flow

**0–5 min | Review**  
Ask students to recap Abraham’s reasoning from the previous lesson.

**5–10 min | Vocabulary Lead-in**  
Teach smash, culprit, evidence, admit, arrogant.

**10–18 min | Reading / Listening**  
Read the idol-breaking and questioning sections.

**18–25 min | Sequencing Task**  
Students put event cards in order using first, later, afterwards, finally.

**25–32 min | Argument Analysis**  
Students answer: “What was Abraham trying to prove?”

**32–37 min | Mini Debate**  
Students discuss: “Why did the people feel ashamed but still reject the truth?”

**37–40 min | Exit Ticket**  
Students write one rhetorical question from Abraham’s perspective.

### Teacher Language

- “What was Abraham’s purpose?”  
- “What evidence did he use?”  
- “Why did the people have no answer?”  
- “Can you turn that into a rhetorical question?”  

### Comprehension Questions

- Why did Abraham break the idols?  
- Why did he leave the largest idol unbroken?  
- How did the people respond to his answer?  

### Discussion Points

- Why is it difficult to admit a mistake publicly?  
- What is the difference between power and truth?  
- Why can arrogance stop a person from accepting evidence?  

### Possible Difficulties

- Students may focus only on the action and miss the argument  
- Reported speech may need modeling  
- Rhetorical questions may feel unfamiliar  

### Support Strategies

- Use a “claim-evidence-conclusion” chart  
- Model reported speech transformation  
- Provide question stems  

### Differentiation

- **Fast finishers:** prepare a short courtroom dialogue  
- **Struggling learners:** match events to pictures and key verbs  

### Interactive App Tips

- Use the axe hotspot to discuss the symbolic purpose of the scene  
- Use the biggest idol hotspot to focus on Abraham’s public argument  

### Assessment Tools

- Sequencing accuracy  
- Explanation of purpose  
- Exit ticket rhetorical question  

### Kinesthetic Activity

Students create a human timeline of the event and each student says one sentence in sequence.

### Global Citizenship Link

How can societies respond better when evidence challenges old assumptions?

### Homework / Extension

Write a paragraph explaining why the people felt ashamed but still refused to change.

## Module 5: The Fire, Trust in Allah, and Nimrod

- explain the events of the fire miracle  
- describe Abraham’s trust in Allah under extreme pressure

Use values-based analysis and argument critique. Students should see that the fire episode highlights trust, while the Nimrod episode highlights the limits of human arrogance.

### Grammar / Discourse Focus

Comparison and evaluation:

- Nimrod claimed that …, but Abraham showed that …

### Pronunciation Focus

## Module 6: Migration, Hajar, Ishmael, Sacrifice, and the Ka‘ba

**Timing:** 40 minutes

### Learning Objectives

By the end of the lesson, students will be able to:

- summarize the migration and desert episode  
- explain Hajar’s trust and effort in the search for water  
- describe the significance of Zamzam, sacrifice, and the Ka‘ba  
- connect patience, obedience, and gratitude across the final sections  

### Expected Student Outcomes

Students should be able to:

- retell the events from migration to the building of the Ka‘ba  
- explain why Hajar’s actions show both trust and effort  
- describe the sacrifice episode with sensitivity and accuracy  
- say why the Ka‘ba is central in the story  

### Key Vocabulary

migration, valley, barren, thirst, mercy, Zamzam, sacrifice, obedience, submission, pilgrimage, foundation, sanctuary

### Pedagogical Focus

Use thematic synthesis. Students should connect several episodes through one central theme: trusting Allah while taking responsible action.

### Grammar / Discourse Focus

Synthesis and linking:

- This episode is connected to … because …  
- Hajar trusted Allah; at the same time, she searched for water.  
- The building of the Ka‘ba shows …  

### Pronunciation Focus

- stress in **migration**, **obedience**, **pilgrimage**, **sanctuary**  
- careful pronunciation of **Hajar**, **Ishmael**, **Zamzam**, **Safa**, **Marwa**, **Ka‘ba**

### Materials / App Features

- map or place visuals  
- Zamzam and Ka‘ba visuals  
- hotspots for Safa, Marwa, Zamzam, sacrifice, Ka‘ba  
- timeline worksheet  
- reflection task  

### 40-Minute Lesson Flow

**0–5 min | Warm-up**  
Ask: “What is the difference between waiting and trusting?”

**5–10 min | Vocabulary Preview**  
Teach migration, valley, thirst, mercy, sacrifice, pilgrimage.

**10–18 min | Guided Reading / Listening**  
Focus on Hajar and Ishmael in the valley and the appearance of Zamzam.

**18–24 min | Timeline Task**  
Students order the final events: migration, desert, Zamzam, sacrifice, Ka‘ba.

**24–31 min | Theme Connection**  
Students identify where they see trust, effort, obedience, and gratitude.

**31–36 min | Pair Speaking**  
Students explain one event and one value connected to it.

**36–40 min | Exit Ticket**  
Students write: “Hajar’s story teaches…” and “The Ka‘ba section shows…”

### Teacher Language

- “Where do we see trust?”  
- “Where do we see effort?”  
- “How are these events connected?”  
- “Use one event and one value in your answer.”  

### Comprehension Questions

- Why did Abraham leave Hajar and Ishmael in the valley?  
- What did Hajar do when the water finished?  
- How did Zamzam appear?  
- What did Abraham and Ishmael build?  

### Discussion Points

- Why is Hajar’s effort important?  
- What does sacrifice teach in this story?  
- How does the Ka‘ba connect Abraham’s family story to later generations?  

### Possible Difficulties

- Students may need support connecting several episodes  
- Some vocabulary is religious and abstract  
- Students may summarize without identifying values  

### Support Strategies

- Use a timeline plus a values chart  
- Provide linking phrases  
- Give students one event each before group synthesis  

### Differentiation

- **Fast finishers:** prepare a one-minute presentation on Zamzam or the Ka‘ba  
- **Struggling learners:** label the timeline with key words and images  

### Interactive App Tips

- Use place hotspots to clarify Safa, Marwa, and the valley  
- Use Zamzam and Ka‘ba hotspots for reflection questions  
- Replay audio for emotional scenes and ask students to identify tone  

### Assessment Tools

- Timeline accuracy  
- Event-value connections  
- Exit ticket reflection  

### Kinesthetic Activity

## 9. DIFFERENTIATION STRATEGIES

### For Stronger Learners

- Ask for evidence-based answers  
- Add comparison or debate tasks  
- Assign short presentations  
- Encourage paragraph writing with topic sentences  
- Ask them to create their own comprehension questions  

### For Learners Who Need Support

- Provide vocabulary lists before reading  
- Use visuals and timelines  
- Give sentence frames  
- Allow pair rehearsal before speaking  
- Reduce text load by focusing on selected chunks  
- Check understanding with short, concrete questions  

### For Mixed-Level Groups

- Use roles: reader, summarizer, vocabulary finder, evidence finder, presenter  
- Let students complete the same task with different output lengths  
- Pair stronger and quieter students carefully  
- Use collaborative charts before individual writing

## 13. FINAL TEACHER NOTES

The Prophet Abraham (as) story is suitable for B2 learners because it allows them to practice advanced comprehension, reasoning, discussion, and reflection without losing the emotional power of narrative. Keep lessons active, respectful, and text-based. Let students speak, question, compare, and write. The goal is for students to understand the story deeply, use English meaningfully, and reflect on the values of Tawheed, courage, trust, patience, sacrifice, and gratitude.
